\section{Results}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
We compare EARS-MMOEA against six baselines (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}): three recent
state-of-the-art methods (CPDEA~\parencite{liu2020cpdea}, MMEA-WI~\parencite{li2021mmeawi},
HREA~\parencite{li2023hrea}) and three classic methods
(MO\_Ring\_PSO\_SCD~\parencite{yue2018moring}, DN-NSGA-II~\parencite{liang2016dnnsga2},
Omni-optimizer~\parencite{deb2008omni}). The baselines are \emph{re-implemented in Python} from
official sources; this is a threat to validity, since an imperfect re-implementation could
under-represent a method, so it is disclosed here. We mitigate it by
validating each port on MMF1--8 against published behaviour and by never weakening a
baseline: each is run with its authors' recommended operators on every problem. All reported
problems (the MMF suite, the CEC extension, and the facility-placement case study) are
box-constrained only, so the constraint-aware selection (M5) and the $\epsilon$-relaxation are
general capabilities that stay inert here (consistent with ablation A6) and are retained for
constrained MMOPs we do not study. We use a \emph{common-budget, common-seed} protocol: identical
population size and evaluation budget for all algorithms, and an algorithm-independent
seed (the seed of run $r$ depends only on the problem and $r$, so every algorithm sees the
same initial sampling), with 30 independent runs. We report IGD, IGD+, normalised HV,
IGDX, PSP, mode coverage, the number of modes and spacing. Among the decision-space metrics we
weight IGDX and PSP (reference-based, parameter-free) and the quality-gated mode coverage above
the raw $\#$modes count, a silhouette-$k$-means cluster count that can over-segment a single
equivalent set. We therefore report $\#$modes as indicative, not as primary evidence.
Statistics follow
\textcite{demsar2006statistical}, with the test basis stated explicitly. \emph{Per metric},
the pairwise EARS-vs-baseline win/tie/loss comes from a Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the \emph{30
paired runs at each problem} (paired by the algorithm-independent seed), Holm-corrected and
aggregated over the eight problems; the Friedman omnibus and its post-hoc instead operate on the
\emph{eight per-problem mean ranks}. We do \emph{not} pool dependent indicators into a single
all-metric rank; instead we report two metric groups (objective-space: IGD, IGD+, HV, spacing;
decision-space: IGDX, PSP, mode coverage), each with its own mean rank. \emph{Primary metrics
are fixed a priori}: for MMOP the defining quality is decision-space coverage of the Pareto
sets, so IGDX and PSP (the standard decision-space indicators) are the primary metrics, with
the objective-space group secondary. This choice is made before seeing results and
applied uniformly to benchmark, ablation and case study, so the case-study headline uses the same
pre-registered metric choice. To keep all mechanisms on an equal footing,
the \emph{common budget is counted in function evaluations}, which dominate runtime; EARS's
auxiliary machinery (archives, bandit, periodic $k$-means) consumes no extra evaluations, so it
confers no hidden budget advantage. Reference Pareto sets/fronts for MMF1--8 are analytic
(transcribed from PlatEMO~\parencite{tian2017platemo} and validated). \emph{Baseline fidelity}: the
six baselines are Python re-implementations run with their authors' recommended operators.
Each is checked to recover the analytic Pareto sets and to reproduce the qualitative ordering
of the source papers (e.g.\ on MMF1 the classics reach IGDX $\approx 0.043$--$0.048$, and
CPDEA its characteristic strong decision-space coverage). HREA is additionally validated to
recover multiple equivalent Pareto sets and to be a strong competitor
(Section~\ref{sec:related}). A number-for-number match to each original paper is not possible
under a single common-budget protocol (the sources use different population sizes, budgets and
encodings); we therefore validate fidelity by analytic recovery and qualitative ordering, and
disclose any re-implementation gap as a threat to validity. With only
eight problems the Friedman power is limited, so rank gaps are read as indicative and always
reported alongside the pairwise tests. Parameters are frozen on a validation subset and used
unchanged everywhere (Section~\ref{sec:param}). All experiments were run on a single
workstation. \emph{Hardware:} Intel Core i9-9900K @ 3.6\,GHz ($8$ cores / $16$ threads),
$32$\,GB DDR4 RAM, $2$\,TB NVMe SSD; the method is CPU-only and uses \emph{no GPU}.
\emph{Software:} Ubuntu 20.04 LTS (Linux 5.15, x86-64), Python $3.13$ with NumPy $2.2.6$,
SciPy $1.16.3$ and scikit-learn $1.8.0$; all random seeds are fixed and algorithm-independent
(\S above). Runs are embarrassingly parallel and dispatched across $14$ single-threaded worker
processes (two threads left for the scheduler), with BLAS pinned to one thread per worker to
avoid oversubscription. A single benchmark run (population $200$, $50{,}000$ evaluations)
completes in roughly $10$--$20$\,s wall-clock, so each $30$-run problem-$\times$-algorithm cell
finishes in a few minutes.

\subsection{Controlled study: placement vs.\ form of the diversity term}
\label{sec:novelty}
Our thesis has two parts: the decision-space sparsity signal $S$ should be confined to
within-front (intra-front) selection rather than allowed into the dominance sort, and the precise
within-front \emph{form} is incidental. We test each with a controlled experiment that holds the
signal, operators, niching and budget fixed and varies only one factor.

\paragraph{The within-front form is secondary} Holding $S$ within the front, the multiplicative
key $E(1{+}\beta S)$ and the additive key $E{+}\beta\,\mathrm{med}(E)\,S$ are statistically
indistinguishable on MMF1--8 (IGD $2/6/0$, $p{=}0.74$; IGDX $2/6/0$, $p{=}0.08$, additive
marginally better on average; Table~\ref{tab:novelty}). The equivalence key also beats a
CPDEA-style penalised-density key used within the front (IGD $8/0/0$). We use the
multiplicative form as the default; the additive form performs equally well.

\paragraph{Placement is the decisive factor} To test placement we hold the
\emph{same} pure sparsity signal $S$ fixed and move only its location: as a within-front
selection key (ours), or fused into the global selection fitness so that $S$ competes with
convergence \emph{across} front boundaries (``in the sort''), where a decision-diverse but
unconverged solution can displace a converged one. Everything else is identical. We run this in
two settings: the full framework, and a minimal skeleton with the auxiliary modules (archives,
cross-mode mating, operator portfolio) removed, so the effect cannot be an artefact of those
modules (Table~\ref{tab:isolation}). The outcome is consistent and holds in both settings:
placing $S$ in the sort degrades \emph{both} convergence (IGD $0.003\!\to\!0.031$, within-front
wins $7/1/0$) and coverage (IGDX $0.038\!\to\!0.107$, $8/0/0$), because it scatters the
population into unconverged decision-diverse regions and loses the Pareto sets themselves.
Confined to the front, the same $S$ improves coverage over no-$S$ at negligible
convergence cost. To test whether the in-sort point was simply weakly tuned, we sweep the in-sort
weight $\lambda$ of the fused key ($\mathrm{score}={-}\mathrm{conv}+\lambda S$) over
$\{0.05,0.1,0.25,0.5,1\}$ and trace the resulting IGD--IGDX curve (Figure~\ref{fig:insort}):
increasing $\lambda$ moves the in-sort point but never reaches the within-front point, which lies
below-left of and \emph{dominates the entire in-sort curve on both axes} (the best in-sort weight,
$\lambda{=}1$, still trails by $4.3\times$ on IGD and $1.7\times$ on IGDX). The placement effect is
thus robust to the in-sort weight, not an artefact of one comparison. This is the contribution: a
pure decision-space-diversity term belongs in within-front selection, not in the dominance sort.

\paragraph{A qualification} The claim is specific to a \emph{pure} diversity signal. A
convergence-\emph{penalised} density key (CPDEA-style, $S/(1{+}\lambda\,\mathrm{conv})$) behaves
differently: because it already discounts poorly-converged solutions, it survives even when placed
in the sort (in-sort vs within-front for that key: IGD $6/0/2$ in the in-sort direction). So it is
not that any diversity-related quantity is harmful in the sort; it is that an \emph{unpenalised}
sparsity signal must be kept out of it. It also follows that wherever CPDEA shows weaker
convergence (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}), that stems from its overall search design, not merely
from where its key sits.

\subsection{Standard benchmark (MMF1--8)}
\label{sec:benchmark}
Having established the placement principle on EARS-Core (Section~\ref{sec:novelty}), we now evaluate
the integrated EARS-Full against the baselines, reporting the two metric groups separately rather
than a single pooled rank (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_rank}).
On the \emph{a-priori primary} decision-space metrics IGDX and PSP, EARS and the density-specialist
CPDEA are statistically tied for the lead (mean rank $1.88$ each). The distributions favour EARS for
stability: across the eight problems EARS's IGDX and PSP rank has median $2.0$ and IQR $[1.8,2.0]$,
versus CPDEA's median $1.5$ and IQR $[1.0,2.2]$. The per-problem Vargha--Delaney effect size confirms
the tie (median $A_{12}{=}0.46$, $[0.12,0.90]$, straddling $0.5$); against every other baseline the
effect on IGDX is large ($A_{12}$ median $0.89$--$1.00$).
At the \emph{group} level, EARS has the best objective-space mean rank ($2.16$) and the second-best
decision-space mean rank ($1.96$, behind CPDEA's $1.83$), and it is the \emph{only} algorithm top-2
in both groups: CPDEA leads decision space but is near-last in objective space ($6.31$), and
Omni-optimizer is the reverse ($2.22$ objective, $4.88$ decision). The balance is decisive: EARS
matches CPDEA's decision-space coverage while winning convergence on every problem (IGD/HV 8/0/0).
The Friedman test, run \emph{per metric} on the eight problem means, is
significant for every metric (e.g.\ IGDX $\chi^2{=}28.0$, $p{=}9.5{\times}10^{-5}$; IGD
$p{=}2.0{\times}10^{-4}$; HV $p{=}5.7{\times}10^{-4}$, $n{=}8$). Against the indicator-based
state-of-the-art method \textbf{MMEA-WI} it wins the defining MMOP metrics IGDX and PSP \textbf{8/0/0}
(win/tie/loss) and IGD 7/0/1, HV 6/1/1. Against the density-based state-of-the-art method \textbf{CPDEA} it
wins IGD and HV \textbf{8/0/0} but only \emph{ties} on IGDX and PSP (2/4/2)
(Table~\ref{tab:benchmark_wtl}); CPDEA reaches that decision-space parity only by
sacrificing convergence (IGD rank 5.25/6). Thus EARS does not out-diversify CPDEA on
IGDX (the two are not separable under the Friedman post-hoc either, Table~\ref{tab:significance});
it matches CPDEA's decision-space coverage while winning convergence on every problem (IGD/HV
8/0/0), a balance not achieved by any evaluated baseline. The recent state-of-the-art method \textbf{HREA} ranks
last on this suite (average rank $5.02/7$): its hierarchy ranking retains local Pareto fronts,
which earns nothing on MMF1--8 (these have global, not deceptive-local, Pareto sets) yet costs
convergence, and EARS beats it $8/0/0$ on IGDX/PSP and $7/1/0$ on IGD/HV. HREA is, however, a
genuinely strong competitor on the harder case study below (second overall), confirming it is
not a weak baseline. Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap} summarizes the per-metric average ranks, and
Figure~\ref{fig:mmf5} shows EARS recovering both equivalent Pareto sets of MMF5.

\paragraph{Held-out problems (no tuning overlap)} The hyperparameters are frozen on the
validation subset MMF1/MMF2/MMF5 (Section~\ref{sec:param}), so MMF1--8 is not a clean test set. We
therefore re-read the result on the five \emph{held-out} problems MMF3/4/6/7/8, which never
participated in tuning. The conclusion is unchanged: EARS has the best all-metric average rank
($2.45$ of seven; next MO\_Ring\_PSO\_SCD $3.10$) and is again top-2 in both spaces (IGDX/PSP
$2.20$, IGD/HV $2.40$), winning no single metric outright (CPDEA edges IGDX/PSP, Omni-optimizer
IGD/HV) but leading the balanced average. The full MMF1--8 numbers are reported as descriptive,
and the headline pattern does not depend on the tuned problems.

\paragraph{Deceptive problems: an observed scope boundary} The benchmarks above (and the
case study) share one feature: their decision-space diversity comes from \emph{multiple
equivalent global} Pareto sets. To probe the opposite regime we add the deceptive scalable
problems MMF10--12, whose decision space has one or two \emph{global} Pareto sets plus higher
\emph{local} basins (numerically-validated global references; the local optima are correctly
excluded). Here \textbf{EARS-MMOEA is \emph{not} rank-1}: on the convention-primary metric IGDX it is
\emph{fourth}, losing $0/1/2$ to MMEA-WI; on the all-metric average it is third
(rank $3.46$ of seven, behind MMEA-WI $2.92$ and MO\_Ring\_PSO\_SCD $3.04$;
Table~\ref{tab:deceptive_rank}). The reason is exactly our inductive
bias: EARS \emph{preserves} decision-space-diverse solutions, which is a virtue when that
diversity is equivalent global optima but a liability under deception, where it retains
solutions sitting in suboptimal local basins; methods that converge harder (MMEA-WI, MO\_Ring)
avoid the traps and score better. We report this prominently because it sharply delimits the
claim: EARS is for multimodality arising from \emph{equivalent global} Pareto sets, not for
deception, and the gap is small in absolute IGDX ($0.005$ vs $0.004$) but consistent in rank.
HREA, whose hierarchy ranking is built for local fronts, correspondingly improves to rank-2 on
IGDX here, confirming both its competence and the regime split.

\subsection{High-dimensional study: a corollary of the placement}
\label{sec:highdim}
The placement makes a falsifiable high-dimensional prediction: because convergence rides the
dominance rank and not the diversity signal, it should be the most robust to dimension as the
distance-based diversity estimate loses contrast. We test this.
The MMF suite is two-dimensional in decision space, so it cannot probe whether the $k$NN
sparsity estimate and the silhouette $k$-means niching degrade as the decision dimension grows
(a known concern for distance-based methods). We therefore build a scalable problem,
\emph{ScalableMMF2}, that embeds the two equivalent Pareto sets of MMF2 in $d$ dimensions by
appending $d{-}2$ spherical distance variables that vanish on the Pareto set; the number of
equivalent global sets stays fixed at two and the reference is analytic at every $d$ (Pareto-set
image matches $f_2{=}1{-}\sqrt{f_1}$ exactly and is non-dominated against a $4{\times}10^4$
decision cloud). We run all seven algorithms at $d\in\{5,10,30,50,100\}$ under the frozen
protocol, with the budget held fixed across $d$ and identical for every algorithm
(Table~\ref{tab:highdim}).

The outcome both delimits and reinforces the central claim. \textbf{(i) A scope boundary:}
absolute IGDX grows with $d$ for every method (none fully recovers both sets at $d{=}100$), so the
diversity machinery does lose resolution in high dimensions. The mechanism is measurable: the
coefficient of variation of the $k$NN distances in a uniform population falls from $0.20$ at
$d{=}5$ to $0.03$ at $d{=}100$, so the sparsity signal $S$ (a normalised $k$NN distance) loses
contrast as $d$ grows, which is why every method's coverage degrades. \textbf{(ii) The placement helps
exactly here:} at low $d$ the density-specialist CPDEA has the best IGDX ($0.05$/$0.15$ at
$d{=}5$/$10$, the main-benchmark tie), but EARS degrades the most gracefully and is best on
\emph{both} IGDX and IGD at $d{\ge}50$, the margin widening with $d$ ($d{=}100$: IGDX $0.89$ vs
next $1.52$; IGD $0.65$ vs next $1.30$). The reason is structural and matches our thesis: because
EARS carries convergence in the dominance rank and keeps the diversity signal out of it, its
convergence is insulated precisely where the distance-based diversity estimates lose contrast,
whereas methods that fold diversity into the fitness (CPDEA, MMEA-WI) see convergence collapse
($\mathrm{IGD}{>}2$ at $d{=}100$). Across the sweep EARS has the best average rank on the primary
decision-space metric IGDX ($2.0$) and on every convergence metric (IGD $1.8$, HV $1.4$), losing
IGDX only to CPDEA and only at low $d$ ($2/1/2$ over the five dimensions). We report the full
texture: with only five dimension points the Friedman power is limited (IGDX $p{=}0.09$, IGD
$p{=}0.06$, HV $p{=}0.008$), and EARS trails CPDEA on PSP ($3.2$ vs $2.8$); the average-rank lead
and the high-$d$ crossover are nonetheless consistent.

\paragraph{A second scalable family} To check the high-dimensional finding is not specific to one
constructed problem, we add a structurally different scalable family, \emph{ScalableDMP}: a
distance-minimisation MMOP with two equivalent \emph{segment} Pareto sets and a linear front
(analytic reference verified), embedded in $d$ dimensions the same way
(Table~\ref{tab:highdim_dmp}). The two families agree on the main claim and differ on the rest. \textbf{Convergence insulation generalizes:} EARS has the best IGD average
rank ($1.6$ of seven) and degrades the most gracefully by a wide margin (at $d{=}100$, IGD $0.36$
versus $4.1$ for the next method and $6$--$10$ for the rest), exactly as on ScalableMMF2.
\textbf{The coverage advantage is landscape-dependent:} on this distance-segment geometry EARS is
only third on IGDX average rank ($2.8$, behind MO\_Ring\_PSO\_SCD $1.4$ and CPDEA $2.0$), leading
on IGDX only at $d{=}100$ as the others collapse. We therefore state the high-dimensional claim at
the level the evidence supports: the within-front placement makes \emph{convergence} the most
robust to dimension across both families, while the size of the \emph{coverage} benefit depends on
the problem's decision-space geometry.

\subsection{Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}
We ablate ten variants on MMF1--8 (Table~\ref{tab:ablation_rank}, Figure~\ref{fig:ablation}).
For MMOP the relevant signal is \emph{decision-space} quality (IGDX, PSP, mode coverage):
pure-convergence indicators (IGD, HV) reward a population that \emph{collapses} onto a single
mode (useless for MMOP), so we report them but do not average them together with the
decision-space metrics (Table~\ref{tab:ablation_rank} groups the two blocks). The trade-off is
visible directly in the table: stripping the diversity machinery improves the
\emph{convergence}-block mean but destroys the \emph{decision-space} block (e.g.\ backbone-only
A8 has the best convergence mean yet the worst decision-space mean, $10.0$), which is exactly
the mode collapse EARS is built to avoid.

Measured where MMOP quality lives, \textbf{the full method is never beaten by any variant}: it
has \emph{zero} losses on IGDX and on mode coverage against all nine variants (Wilcoxon).
Removing the equivalence backbone (A8) worsens IGDX by $6.2\times$ ($623\%$, 8/0/0), the equivalence fitness (A3)
$181\%$ (8/0/0), and the operator portfolio (A7) $35\%$ (5/3/0); the advertised \emph{sparsity
bonus} (A9, removing $S$) adds a smaller, targeted $+11\%$ IGDX (A0 vs A9 $3/5/0$),
Holm-significant on the harder close-mode instances and a tie on the rest. The decision-mode
archive A1, structure-family archive A2 and constraint-aware selection A6 are statistical ties on
the benchmark (0/8/0): A2 and A6 target routing and constrained settings we do not study, and
A1's contribution is not separable on the two-dimensional MMF problems. We are therefore explicit that EARS's benchmark strength is
driven by the equivalence-aware framework, with $S$ a targeted addition on hard multimodal
problems; and that no component's removal beats the full method on the metrics that define MMOP.

\paragraph{Forward construction: where the benchmark gain is built} The subtractive ablation
above answers ``what does removing a module cost''; to answer the complementary
``where does the gain come from'' (and to address the concern that performance is an artefact of
stacking seven modules) we also run a \emph{constructive} ladder
(Table~\ref{tab:forward_ablation}): starting from an NSGA-II-like base, each rung adds exactly one
capability. The picture is clear. Decision-space quality is built by three components:
the equivalence key ($+E$: IGDX $0.269\!\to\!0.060$), the within-front sparsity term
($+S$, the core selection mechanism: $0.060\!\to\!0.050$), and the operator portfolio
($0.050\!\to\!0.037$). Adding cross-mode mating and the decision-mode archive then
leaves the unconstrained benchmark unchanged ($0.037$), consistent with those being
modules for routing and constrained settings not studied here. Crucially, \emph{convergence is essentially flat along the entire ladder} (IGD
$0.0027\!\to\!0.0029$, a $\approx 7\%$ change while IGDX improves $7\times$): the whole
decision-space stack is added at a small, measured convergence cost, the constructive counterpart
of the within-front placement argument. The ladder endpoints reproduce the subtractive A8 (base)
and A0 (full) numerically, an internal consistency check. The benchmark strength is therefore
located in the core selection idea, not in module count.

\subsection{Parameter analysis}
\label{sec:param}
We sweep the core hyperparameter, the sparsity weight $\beta$, on the validation subset
(MMF1/MMF2/MMF5, 30 runs each; Figure~\ref{fig:beta}). The decision-space IGDX improves
monotonically with $\beta$ (mean $0.045\!\to\!0.041$), with most of the gain captured by
$\beta=0.5$; convergence (IGD) is essentially flat up to $\beta=0.5$ and then \emph{significantly}
regresses for $\beta\ge 1$ (paired Wilcoxon vs $\beta{=}0.5$: $p{=}0.002$ on MMF5 and $p{=}0.05$
on MMF1, while MMF2 stays robust). We therefore freeze $\beta=0.5$ as the \emph{knee}: it
secures most of the decision-space coverage at a small, non-significant convergence cost,
whereas $\beta\ge 1$ buys little extra IGDX while significantly degrading IGD on two of three
problems. $\beta$ is thus genuinely sensitive, and we report this; the choice is validation-only. The remaining parameters were chosen by a
one-factor-at-a-time sweep on the same subset (Figure~\ref{fig:param}), with a full-budget

\subsection{OpenStreetMap-network case study: real-map facility placement}
\label{sec:placement}
We now test the selection principle on a genuinely multimodal real-network case study. In this
setting, the design task is to present several near-equivalent station layouts for later planning
judgement. This is the decision-support role that within-front diversity is intended to preserve.
We frame this as a computational design case study on real road networks rather than a fully
specified operational emergency-planning system: the graphs are real OpenStreetMap city networks,
but the demand points are sampled and the objectives are the two classical access objectives,
without population weights, capacities, or existing-facility data. We place $K=5$ stations for
$M=150$ OpenStreetMap demand nodes
(bi-objective: mean vs max access) over three demand instances $\times$ 30 runs with the same
protocol and baselines. \emph{The instances are genuinely multimodal}: on Macau, pooling EARS's
non-dominated layouts and comparing them with the exact Hungarian set distance, there are
$16$--$18$ pairs that are objective-near-equivalent (within $2\%$ of the normalised range) yet
geographically distinct (set distance $\ge 0.05$--$0.10$ of the normalised extent), i.e.\ multiple
near-equivalent placement families rather than one optimum re-encoded. \emph{Reference sets:} as
the true PF/PS are unknown, the per-instance reference front/set are the non-dominated union of all
algorithms' feasible outputs (standard practice, applied identically to every algorithm). Because
this union includes every algorithm's own output, it can in principle flatter all contributors
equally. We note this as a residual bias and partially control it through the
algorithm-independent seeding and the Hungarian robustness check. We first detail Macau, then test generalisation on four
further cities. Over the \emph{full seven-metric suite} EARS-MMOEA has the best
average rank, \textbf{3.17} of seven (Table~\ref{tab:placement_rank}; next:
MO\_Ring\_PSO\_SCD $3.24$); the margin over the strongest baseline is narrow on the full
suite. On the case-study \emph{core suite}, the three reference-based indicators (HV, IGD, IGDX,
fixed a priori for this instance type and led by the primary decision-space indicator IGDX;
$\#$modes is reported but \emph{excluded} from the core suite, because the search and that metric
share a silhouette-$k$-means geometry), EARS's lead widens to $2.44$ (next,
MO\_Ring\_PSO\_SCD, $2.78$). EARS
attains the best mean HV ($0.77$) and IGD and the strongest multimodality, finding on average
$14.7$ \emph{layout modes}, more than any baseline. Here a layout mode is a silhouette-$k$-means
cluster (capped at $20$) of the obtained non-dominated set. This indicative count differs from
the $2$--$5$ niches the search maintains internally and from a benchmark's true Pareto-set branch
count, and it can over-segment a single equivalent family. The density-based CPDEA, by
contrast, degrades to last (average rank $5.86$; $\approx 2.0$ modes, mean HV $0.25$): its
convergence-only DE concentrates on a single layout on a problem with many equivalent
optima, illustrating precisely the regime an MMOP method targets. On this single city
Friedman is significant on $\#$modes ($p{=}0.013$) but, at only $n{=}3$ instances, not on IGDX
($p{=}0.065$) or HV ($p{=}0.19$), which we report; the across-city test below is far stronger. Figure~\ref{fig:placement} shows, on
the real OpenStreetMap map, the Pareto-equivalent station-layout families found by EARS; the
decision-space-diversity gap versus the baselines, across all five cities, is in
Figure~\ref{fig:placement_cities}.

\paragraph{Generalisation to five real cities} To check the result is not specific to one
city, we repeat the entire study (identical frozen protocol, same seven algorithms, three
demand instances $\times$ 30 runs each) on four further real OpenStreetMap cities (Guangzhou,
Shenzhen, San Francisco, Hong Kong) downloaded with the same code path, giving five real
cities in total. Each city is treated as an independent benchmark and ranked separately;
Table~\ref{tab:placement_multicity} reports the full-suite average rank per city and, in the
bottom row, the mean rank \emph{across all five cities}. \textbf{Averaged over the five
cities, EARS-MMOEA is rank-1 with a clear margin}, most decisively on the core MMOP suite
(HV, IGD, IGDX; the reference-based indicators): core-suite average rank $1.94$ versus the
next-best, HREA, at $3.21$; on the full seven-metric suite the lead is $2.71$ vs $3.37$, with the
secondary access indicators narrowing it. (The runner-up differs by aggregation level:
MO\_Ring\_PSO\_SCD or Omni-optimizer per single city, HREA on the five-city average, so no single
baseline is consistently second.) Per city, EARS is rank-1 on the full suite of
\emph{all five} cities and on the core suite of three (Macau, Guangzhou, San~Francisco), with
the lead widening on the larger, more multimodal grids (Guangzhou $2.19$ full / $1.11$ core,
San~Francisco $2.67$ / $1.44$ core). An \emph{across-city} Friedman (treating the five cities as
blocks, run \emph{separately per metric} since the indicators are correlated and should not be
pooled into one inflated block count) is significant on every core metric (HV $p{=}0.003$, IGD
$p{=}0.001$, IGDX $p{=}0.005$), with EARS at the best mean rank
on each, confirming the five-city lead is not an artefact of any single $n{=}3$ instance
set. The convergence-only CPDEA is last on every city and on the average ($6.0$/$6.7$ full/core),
reproducing the single-layout collapse, while HREA is the strongest competitor (second
overall). We report the two closest cases transparently: on the \emph{core} suites of Shenzhen
($2.17$ vs Omni-optimizer $2.06$) and Hong~Kong ($2.56$ vs $2.22$) Omni-optimizer edges EARS,
though EARS is rank-1 on the full suite of both and on the five-city average.

\paragraph{Exact set-matching distance} The decision-space metrics compare $K$-station layouts
through a canonical-coordinate sort, which is a surrogate for the exact permutation-invariant set
distance. To check the result is not an artefact of that surrogate, we re-evaluate IGDX on the
saved layouts with the exact Hungarian (minimum-cost assignment) distance, which is cheap at
$K{=}5$. The per-city IGDX rank is \emph{identical} under both distances on all five cities, and
EARS keeps the best five-city mean IGDX rank ($1.40$ under either distance; next $3.00$); only the
absolute values shrink, as the optimal matching is shorter than the sorted-coordinate distance.
The ranking is therefore robust to the choice of layout distance.
Figure~\ref{fig:placement_cities} shows the real-map decision-space-diversity gap on
\emph{all five} compared cities.
